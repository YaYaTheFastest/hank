A first principle is a truth you cannot usefully break down further in this problem. Not a slogan. Not “how we do it.” The bedrock. Then you reason up.

Left: analogy — copy the house, iterate the trim. Right: first principles — take it to bricks, then decide if you even want a house.

Reasoning by analogy

“This worked for them.” “That’s how it’s always been done.” “Everyone does X.” You start with what exists and make a small variation. Fast. Cheap. Mentally easy. Bound by someone else’s furniture.

Reasoning from first principles

Name what you are sure is true. Discard the rest as assumption until proven. Build a new structure from those pieces. Slow. Expensive in attention. The only way to get a non-linear answer.

Jay (@jaynitx) put it simply: you don’t start with what exists or what others are doing. You start with what’s fundamentally true and work forward. His own miss: after dropping out he assumed he had to be on every platform. First-principles cut: what am I trying to do, who actually cares, where do those people already spend time? Mostly X. Maybe YouTube. Not Instagram for that niche.

Tim Urban’s picture (Wait But Why): the cook follows a recipe. The chef starts from raw ingredients. Most of life should be cook work. The chef is for the problem that will punish a copied recipe.

The five moves

I name the problem · II strip the fog · III keep only bedrock · IV rebuild · V seal it with a test.

1 Name the job→ 2 Strip assumptions→ 3 Keep irreducibles→ 4 Rebuild→ 5 Falsify
  1. Name the job in one sentence with no jargon. If you cannot, you are still in someone else’s frame.
  2. Strip assumptions. Write every “because.” Circle the ones that are tradition, status, or fear.
  3. Keep irreducibles. Physics, cash, time, a named person, a law, a customer truth you can point at. One or two levels deeper than the room is usually enough. You do not need atoms.
  4. Rebuild from those pieces only. What would you construct if the current product did not exist?
  5. Falsify. What observation would kill this rebuild? If you cannot name one, you built a belief, not a model.

Two tools for the strip: Socratic questions (below) and the Five Whys — keep asking until you hit “I don’t know, let’s look it up” instead of “because I said so.”

When to spend the energy

Musk: it takes more mental energy. Jay is honest that it is not always the right method. Use the gate.

Cook / analogy

Reversible. Cheap if wrong. The recipe is known. Speed matters more than originality. Daily ops, a known meeting, a proven form.

Chef / first principles

Non-linear payoff. High cost of being wrong. The industry story feels stale. You are inventing, not iterating. Someone is about to spend real money or years.

Do not fake it

Calling a preference a “first principle” is cosplay. If it cannot survive “what would change my mind,” it is branding.

Worked cuts

Rockets (SpaceX). Analogy: rockets cost what they have always cost. First principles: what is a rocket made of — aerospace aluminum, some titanium, copper, carbon fiber — and what do those materials cost on the commodity market? Musk’s figure: about 2% of the typical rocket price. The gap is process, tradition, and markup, not physics. Source: Musk, as quoted by Farnam Street / Wired.
Batteries (Tesla, 2012 Kevin Rose interview). Analogy: “packs are $600/kWh and always will be.” First principles: cobalt, nickel, aluminum, carbon, polymer separator, steel can — London Metal Exchange spot. His number then: about $80/kWh in materials. The work is clever combination, not accepting history as a law. Treat $600 and $80 as his 2012 working numbers, not today’s quote.
Distribution (jaynitx). Analogy: be on every platform. First principles: what am I trying to achieve, who is the audience, where do they already spend time? Cut Instagram. Keep the channel that matches the physics of attention.
BuzzFeed / CD Baby (Farnam Street). Peretti: the irreducible of a web hit is distribution people will actually share, not “articles they should read.” Sivers: the irreducible of the business was a thrilled customer, not the office or the raise.
Feynman’s warning. People learn by rote. Knowledge that cannot be rebuilt from pieces is fragile. If you cannot explain it to a child without the slogan, you do not have a principle. You have a costume.

Strip

Five Whys. The last honest line is the one that matters.

Bedrock

Farnam Street: irreducibles in context. Not the atom unless you need the atom.

What is it made of, what does that actually cost, who actually has to show up?

Rebuild and test

Keep a piece only if it survives, not because it is familiar.

No falsifier = belief. No named owner = theater.

Traps

  • First-principles theater. Fancy words, same copy of last year’s plan.
  • Going to atoms when Lego is enough. One or two layers deeper than the room. Stop there unless the physics demands more.
  • Confusing preference with principle. “I like this vendor” is not bedrock.
  • Using it on reversible chores. You will burn the day and call it rigor.
  • Inventing numbers to look like Musk. If you do not have the material cost, you do not have the cut. Write “unknown” and go get it.
  • Because I said so. That is the moment the room is hiding ignorance. Stay in the question.

Related, not the same: Musk’s later five-step algorithm (make requirements less dumb, delete, simplify, accelerate, automate — in that order). That is operations after the principle is found. Do not automate a lie.
